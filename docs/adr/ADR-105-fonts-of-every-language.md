# ADR-105 — Fonts of every language: what a deck is offered, what the library has, and what a design keeps

Status: Implemented in the 2026-10-17 working tree; **not committed** at the time of this record. The fonts and the converted designs are in the author copy of the library and **published** (deployment 88859198 of slidr-media). It builds on [ADR-098](ADR-098-content-in-every-language.md), whose fonts were the Latin and Hebrew families with the subsets of other scripts they happened to have.

## Request

"In the app, show only fonts for the language and for English; in the library, show only fonts for the language. Add more usable fonts for Hebrew and other languages. For text in ready-made slides, existing and future, use the closest matching fonts in style, and include this in the IDE and studio instructions." The user chose: a deck is offered the families of its language and those made for Latin letters (no Hebrew family in an Arabic or English deck); Japanese, Korean and Chinese families with display faces, though ADR-098 had rejected them for their size; the author copy written and published.

## Context

- The library had 23 families. Rubik was the only one with Arabic letters and Poppins the only one with Devanagari; none had Japanese, Korean or Chinese, which fell back to the system's faces.
- The designs of Elements written in sixteen languages (ADR-098, and more by another session while this was done: 56 authored and 18 managed when it was published) were set, in Arabic, wholly in Rubik and, in Hindi, wholly in Poppins: a title in Playfair Display and a caption in Inter became one family. Their Japanese, Korean and Chinese text named Latin families and was drawn in the system's; their Vietnamese named Rubik, which has no Vietnamese letters.
- A family's catalog entry said only which scripts it has. A Hebrew family has Latin letters for the English words of a Hebrew text, a Japanese one Cyrillic letters, so "has the letters" could not tell what a family is for.
- The foreign variants of the authored designs live only in `elements/designs/index.json` (the packs draw Hebrew and English); those of the left-to-right languages have the element ids of the English slide, the Arabic ones those of the Hebrew slide they mirror. The managed designs' variants have ids of their own, in the same order.

## Decision

- **The catalog says what a family is made for, and its counterparts.** `scripts/gen-builtin-fonts.mjs` gives each family `for` (the scripts it was made for) and a character (`kind`: sans, humanist, plex, geometric, soft, grotesk, wide, heavy, condensed, rounded, serif, display serif, heavy serif, hand, mono). From the kind it writes `counterparts`: for each script the family lacks, the family of those letters closest to it in character (Playfair Display → Frank Ruhl Libre, Amiri, Noto Serif Devanagari, Noto Serif JP/KR/SC); a family whose kind's row does not fit it has a row of its own over it (DM Sans → Manrope in Cyrillic and Vietnamese, of its width, where Montserrat ran a fifth wider and overflowed). Each counterpart is checked to have the letters. Counterparts have the bold weights titles need: Tiro Devanagari Hindi, ZCOOL XiaoWei and Rajdhani, with one or narrow weights, are in the library but are no family's counterpart. The neutral grotesques' Arabic is IBM Plex Sans Arabic: Noto Kufi Arabic, measured, runs 14–23% wider than the Rubik the Arabic text was written in, and overflowed boxes that do not wrap. A family of one face that looks bold (Secular One, Suez One, DM Serif Display, Lalezar, Yatra One, Dela Gothic One, Black Han Sans) says so (`looks: 700`), and its counterpart sets the text bold.
- **53 families more, 76 in all with Be Vietnam Pro (below), every one under the OFL**: Hebrew (Miriam Libre, Bellefair, Playpen Sans Hebrew; Arimo, Tinos, Bona Nova, Fredoka, Amatic SC, Cousine, made for Latin with Hebrew), Latin and Cyrillic (Golos Text, Unbounded, Oswald, Lora), Arabic (IBM Plex Sans Arabic, Noto Kufi Arabic, Cairo, Tajawal, Readex Pro, Alexandria, Noto Naskh Arabic, Amiri, El Messiri, Reem Kufi, Lalezar, Baloo Bhaijaan 2, Playpen Sans Arabic), Devanagari (Noto Sans and Serif Devanagari, IBM Plex Sans Devanagari, Mukta, Hind, Rajdhani, Teko, Tiro Devanagari Hindi, Yatra One, Baloo 2, Playpen Sans Deva), Japanese (Noto Sans and Serif JP, Zen Maru Gothic, Dela Gothic One, Klee One), Korean (Noto Sans and Serif KR, Black Han Sans, Jua, Gaegu) and Simplified Chinese (Noto Sans and Serif SC, ZCOOL XiaoWei, ZCOOL KuaiLe, Ma Shan Zheng). A Japanese, Korean or Chinese face comes as about a hundred numbered slices, each with its `unicode-range`, fetched only when text uses it; of the static ones only the weights used are shipped. The fonts folder grows from 5 to 75 MB and the catalog to 1.6 MB.
- **A language's families** (`familyOfLanguage`, `packages/model/src/languages.ts`): those with its letters that were made for them, or for Latin letters (whose families carry Cyrillic and Vietnamese ones too). A catalog without `for` is read by a family's first script.
- **The app offers a deck its language's families and English's** (`familyForDeck`), its language's first, in the font picker and in the settings' list of the library. A face with the deck's letters shows a phrase in the deck's language (a localized equivalent of "Font style") where it showed one in Hebrew; the picker's `hebrew` mark became `sample`. A font of the user's or of the computer is known only to have Hebrew or not, and is offered as before. A deck in a language no content is written in is offered every family.
- **The studio's library shows only the language's families**, with their count; the toggle between all and fitting is gone.
- **Designs keep their character in every language** (`src/elements/designFonts.ts`). Each run of a foreign variant is set from the run its source sets the same text in (the English slide; the Hebrew one for a right-to-left language), paired by element id, else in order: the source's family where it has the language's letters, else its counterpart, at the nearest weight the family has. A run without the letters of a script unlike Latin (a year, a word in English) keeps a family made for Latin letters, as the Hebrew design kept its Latin figures. `scripts/design-fonts.mjs` applies it to `index.json` and `managed.json`, and `designs.test.ts` holds every design to it.
- **The instructions.** `AGENTS.md` says to set each language in a family with its letters and to take the counterpart where the English (for Arabic, the Hebrew) text's family lacks them, never one family for everything. The studio lists, for the request's languages, their families and the counterparts of every family the English and Hebrew files may use (`instructions.py`), and reads `AGENTS.md` as before.
- The agent's template fonts (`TEMPLATE_FONTS`) list the new Hebrew and Latin families; a theme's pair is still `{ he, latin }`, so the families made for other letters are not offered to a theme.
- **Templates and every deck: the theme's fonts reach every language** (second round, the same day). The renderer's `fontStack(pair, lang)` adds, for a deck in a language whose letters the theme's Latin family lacks, that family's counterpart (else the Hebrew family's), which the host tells it from its catalog (`setFontCounterparts`, `familyForLetters` in the model; the app and the studio register it when they read their fonts). An Arabic deck in a theme of Inter draws its letters in IBM Plex Sans Arabic and its figures and English words in Inter; a Japanese deck in a serif theme draws in Noto Serif JP. For Vietnamese and Russian the counterpart comes before the Latin family and draws the whole text: Vietnamese is Latin letters with marks, and Poppins drew a word's plain letters while the counterpart drew its marked ones. Hebrew keeps the pair's own family. No schema change: the pair stays `{ he, latin }`, and an older app draws as before. The HTML export embeds the counterpart like any face the text uses, since it reads the computed stack. The seven templates written in sixteen languages (Zerem, Shvil, Tzuk, Lavan, Shidur, Kod, Atlas) had no run fonts, and drew Arabic, Devanagari, Japanese, Korean and Chinese (and Cyrillic and Vietnamese in some themes) in the system's faces.
- **Line breaks of Japanese and Korean.** With their own faces, the samples' lines broke where the browser breaks them by default: between any two characters, so a name lost its last kana to the next line and a line opened with a small tsu or prolonged sound mark. The slide root sets `line-break: strict` (no small kana, prolonged sound mark or closing punctuation opens a line, in Japanese and Chinese), `word-break: auto-phrase` for a Japanese deck (lines break between phrases) and `keep-all` for a Korean one (between words, at the spaces Korean writes), with `overflow-wrap: break-word` so a phrase longer than its line still breaks. Hebrew, English and the other languages are drawn as before. Chinese breaks between characters, as it is set.
- **Serif counterparts in Cyrillic and Vietnamese** are Tinos, of Frank Ruhl Libre's width: Lora, the first choice, ran 7% wider and broke Tzuk's titles onto another line.
- **The samples' text, where the review found it** (in the catalog and in the template's source, `archive/templates-source/<id>/<lang>.ts`): Lavan in Japanese (a partner's name without its ＝, the bios and two heads in shorter lines); Tzuk in Japanese and Shvil in Japanese (a point and a card a line too long once broken by phrase); Tzuk's first section title in Vietnamese, German, French, Italian, Spanish and Portuguese, which left one word alone on a second line of display serif (localized equivalents as short as "Q3 in Brief"). Titles of other templates whose last line is one word, as some of the English ones are (the Spanish equivalent of "How / we work" beside "How we work"), were left as written.
- **Be Vietnam Pro**, a geometric sans made for Vietnamese, joins the library (76 families) as the Vietnamese counterpart of Poppins, Montserrat and their kind: Montserrat, the first choice, ran wider and broke a Shidur title onto a third line.
- **The interface in Japanese** names Yu Gothic UI before `system-ui` (`packages/ui/src/theme.css`): Chromium otherwise falls back to Yu Gothic, whose kana are a full em wide, a quarter wider than the face Windows draws its own interface in. For Arabic, Hindi, Korean and Chinese the faces `system-ui` falls back to are the system's interface faces already.
- The studio's instructions for translating a template (`template_prompts.fonts_note`) say which counterpart draws the letters the theme lacks, and to name no font; the review page takes `&fallback=system` to draw a deck as before, and reads `images/designs/sizes.json` as the app does (without it, a cropped picture such as `icebox`'s ice was drawn wrong in the review only).

## Considered and rejected

- **One family per language for every text** (as before). It is what the request asked to undo.
- **Choosing by `scripts` alone.** Heebo would be an English font and Noto Sans JP a Russian one.
- **A counterpart table per family written by hand.** Seventy-five rows of nine scripts drift; a kind per family and a row per kind is checked by the generator.
- **Moving a design's Latin figures into the counterpart too.** The Hebrew designs set their years and numbers in Latin families; the other languages keep that.

- **A text box whose family changed shrinks to fit** (`autoFit: 'shrink'` where it was `none`): its text was written to the measure of the family it had. The renderer's shrink fits the height only, so a box that keeps its text on one line, and a shape, are left to the review; three were fitted there by hand (the Japanese chip of `m-opensource` widened to 440, the Chinese time line of `m-studio-live-stream` to 300, the Japanese date of `m-techmeetup` set at 27).

## Verification

- Unit tests: the model's language rule, the conversion (`designFonts.test.ts`), the designs' contract with the new rule that every run of a language but Hebrew and English is set in a family with its letters or keeps a Latin family for a text without them, the agent's template fonts, the translations. Against the published library after publishing: 740 passed; the two that fail failed before it (the managed `invitations` group has one design; `ar/statement` leaves a text over its subject). The studio's 37 server tests pass.
- E2E (`editor-fonts-picker.spec.ts`, `shell-fonts.spec.ts`), against a dev server reading the staged library: 26 passed, among them an Arabic deck offered Rubik and the Arabic families first with an Arabic sample, and no Heebo. The picker was also captured in Arabic, Japanese, Hindi, Russian and English decks and looked at.
- Every design in each of the fourteen languages that changed was rendered before (the published library) and after (the staged one), in sheets of six at 960 px and in thumbnail sheets, with the review page's measures of text out of its box and the design check, and the two compared. The first round found what the decisions above answer: Noto Kufi Arabic and Tajawal overflowing boxes and chips that do not wrap, Montserrat overflowing Russian and Vietnamese, titles from one-face heavy families set thin, three single lines and a chip too wide. After them, the conversion adds no text out of its frame; what stays is line boxes of the taller Arabic, Devanagari and East Asian faces reaching a few pixels past frames whose letters fit, and the shrink of one Japanese, Korean and Chinese title to 96%.
- Found before the conversion, in designs another session wrote while this was done, and fixed in the second round: the dashed line of `m-paneltrain` crossed its station labels in every language (the three lower stations' labels raised 15–30px); the title of the Arabic `m-hackathon` ran over the clock (Arabic for "From idea to presentation", both lines at 104px); the red ball of the Arabic `statement` sat away from the subject point, the last word apart from it (Arabic for "More stories" reaches it, the ball back at the subject point, the lines 40px further apart for Arabic's ascenders). `icebox` was whole all along: the review page drew its cropped ice without the picture's size.
- Second round (templates, the interface, line breaks): the seven templates' samples in all fourteen languages were rendered with the system's faces (`&fallback=system`) and with the counterparts, compared, and the slides the comparison and a measure of each line flagged rendered at full size and looked at; after the fixes no text of any of them is past its box or over another (L01, L03, L06), and Japanese and Korean break no name or word. The design check's other findings in Atlas (alignment within 6px, L09/L10) are in every language alike, the unchanged Latin ones too, and came with that template's own republication. The interface was captured in Arabic, Hindi, Japanese, Korean, Chinese and Russian. Unit tests against the published library: 1017 passed, the same two failing as before; browser tests of the renderer and the fonts: 88 passed (one, Arabic and Japanese drawn in their counterparts, new); the studio's server tests: 37 passed. The second publication (deployment 2f8b0220) added Be Vietnam Pro's 37 files and changed the four catalogs, and nothing else.
- Publishing: the dry run against the published library showed 2223 files added, all under `fonts/`, three changed (`fonts/catalog.json`, `elements/designs/index.json`, `elements/designs/managed.json`) and none removed, and the author copy's catalogs were byte for byte the reviewed ones.
